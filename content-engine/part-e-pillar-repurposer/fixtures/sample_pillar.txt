So the question I get asked more than any other, and I mean by a long way, is
some version of "how bad is this going to be." And I want to answer that
honestly rather than just reassuringly, because I think people can tell the
difference.

Here's what I've noticed after doing this for a while. The people who are most
frightened are almost never frightened of the treatment. They're frightened of
the moment before the treatment. They're frightened of not knowing what's
happening, of someone starting before they're ready, of being in a chair where
they can't talk and can't see. That's the actual fear. And once you understand
that, the whole thing becomes a lot more manageable, because that fear has
solutions and "it might hurt" doesn't really.

So the first thing we do differently, and it costs us nothing, is we tell you
what we're going to do before we do it. Every time. Not a lecture, just: I'm
going to look, then I'm going to tell you what I see, then we'll decide
together. Nobody starts anything in this practice while a patient is still
working out what's about to happen to them.

The second thing is the hand. If you raise your hand, we stop. Not "we finish
this bit and then stop." We stop. I say this to every new patient and I mean it
literally, and the reason I mean it literally is that it only works if you
believe it. If you think I'm going to push through for another thirty seconds,
you'll never raise your hand, and then you're just lying there enduring
something, which is exactly what we're trying to avoid.

Third thing, and this is the one people don't expect. We will tell you when
something doesn't need treating. There's a real temptation in this job to treat
everything you can see, and I think that's how a lot of people end up not
trusting dentists. If I look at something and my honest view is that we should
watch it for six months, I'll say watch it for six months. You'll get that in
writing on the treatment plan. And yes, that means sometimes you come in and
leave having spent nothing, and that's fine.

On the treatment plan itself. Everything gets written down before anything
starts. What we're doing, why we're doing it, what it involves, roughly how
long it takes. You take that away and read it. There's no situation where you're
in the chair and someone tells you about a cost you hadn't heard of. That's not
a policy we're proud of, that should just be normal.

Let me talk about time, because I think people underestimate this. A root canal
is not a quick appointment. Plan for about an hour and a half. Front teeth are
usually quicker than molars because molars have more canals to clean out. I
would much rather you hear that from me now than find out at the forty minute
mark when you thought you were nearly done. Most people's complaint afterwards
is that it was long, not that it was sharp.

Afterwards you'll be tender for a couple of days when you bite. That's normal,
it's like a bruise, it settles. What isn't normal is swelling that keeps
growing, or pain that climbs instead of fading. If that happens, ring us. We
hold emergency slots every weekday morning specifically so there's somewhere for
that call to go.

The other thing I want to say, and I'll say it plainly. If it's been years since
you've been to a dentist, I don't care. I genuinely don't. Nobody here is
keeping score. The people who worry about this the most are usually the ones who
had a bad experience somewhere and then the gap got longer and the longer it
got the more embarrassing it felt to break it. I understand how that happens. It
happens to a lot of people. Come in, we'll have a look, we'll tell you where you
actually are, and then you can decide what you want to do about it.

And one more thing on the first visit. It's mostly a conversation. We look, we
take what we need to see properly, we talk. We're not going to start drilling on
day one because you walked through the door. You'll leave knowing what's going
on in your mouth, which for a lot of people is the first time in a long time.
